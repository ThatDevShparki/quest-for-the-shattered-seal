# Sylara Dawnmere

## What He Remembers

He does not remember her the way he remembers his father. His father is specific. Particular. A collection of details so precise they feel like texture. The way Aeralon held a brush when he was sketching something he found exciting. The specific cadence of his voice when he was about to say something he had been thinking about for hours. The smell of his study. The sound of the journal clasp.

Sylara is not like that. She is not details. She is a feeling.

Warmth is the word he reaches for most often. Not warmth in the abstract, not warmth as a synonym for kindness, but warmth as a physical memory, the particular temperature of being near her, of being held by her, of the way the room felt different when she was in it. Children remember bodies before they remember faces. He remembers her presence the way you remember sunlight, not the sun itself but the feeling of being in it.

He remembers that she prayed. Not as an institutional act, not with ceremony or formality, but quietly and often, in the small moments between other things. He does not remember the words. He remembers the stillness of her when she was doing it, the quality of attention she brought to something invisible, and the way that stillness felt safe to be near rather than excluding.

He remembers being called Zeph. The specific sound of it in her voice, which he cannot reproduce and cannot describe except to say that he has heard his name said ten thousand times since and none of them have sounded quite like that.

These are the things he carries. They are not enough to build a person from. They are enough to know that someone was there and that her absence is a specific shape rather than a general one.

## What His Father Told Him

Aeralon spoke of Sylara the way people speak of things they loved so completely that the loving is still present tense even after the losing. Not with grief exactly, or not only with grief, but with the particular reverence of someone who understood that what they had been given was not ordinary and has been trying to be worthy of it ever since.

He told Zeph they met on the job. A place Aeralon was working, a place Sylara was living. The details beyond this were never specified. Zeph has the impression of somewhere ancient and probably beautiful, the kind of place that draws both archaeologists and people who want to live near something that has survived. He knows it was not a professional introduction. He knows it began as friendship and became something else through the accumulation of time and proximity and the growing recognition that neither of them wanted to be anywhere the other was not.

He told Zeph she was devoted. That her faith was quiet and personal and entirely her own, practiced in the small moments rather than the large ones, present in the way she moved through the world rather than announced in how she presented herself to it. She was a cleric of holy light. This is the institutional description. What it meant in practice, in daily life, in the specifics of how she understood her relationship to the divine, Aeralon described only in impressions. She was protective. She was certain in the way that people are certain when their faith is not an argument they are making but a thing they have simply always known.

He told Zeph she was warm. He said this many times in many different ways across many years. It was the word he returned to most reliably when he was talking about her, and Zeph understands now that Aeralon was not being imprecise. He was being exact. Warmth was not a metaphor for her personality. It was the thing itself. The quality that defined her in the room, that made the room different when she was in it.

He told Zeph about the Dawnmere crest. Or rather he told him that Sylara wore it as her holy symbol and that it was her family's mark and that she never explained it further. He said this without interpretation. He did not say he had asked and been told nothing, though that is the implication. He did not say he understood it and chose not to explain. He reported it as a fact without context and left the context to arrive on its own schedule.

Zeph does not know how much of what his father did not say was ignorance and how much was choice. He is less certain of this than he used to be.

He told Zeph about The Architect. He told him the way he told him other difficult things, matter of factly and with enough detail to satisfy the question without enough to invite the next one. A man came to her bedside the night before she died. He had a warm smile and circular glasses. He gave his name as The Architect when a small curious child asked. He was gone before morning.

Aeralon offered no interpretation of this. He never speculated, never theorized, never expressed his own reaction to what he had witnessed. He reported it as a fact and left it there. Zeph accepted it as a fact and left it there too, filed under things that were strange but did not yet have enough context to be frightening.

He is no longer sure that his father's lack of interpretation was the same as his father having no interpretation. He is no longer sure of several things he used to be sure of about what Aeralon knew.

## What He Does Not Know

Which is most of it.

He does not know where she came from. He has no picture of Sylara before Aeralon, no sense of her origin or her family or the place that made her. The Dawnmere name exists but it connects to nothing specific in Zeph's understanding. No geography. No community. No siblings or parents or cousins or anyone who might remember her from before she was Aeralon's wife and Zeph's mother. She arrived in his understanding already fully formed and then was gone before he was old enough to ask the questions that would have filled in the edges.

He does not know what she knew about her own bloodline. He knows the crest was her family's mark. He knows she wore it throughout her life as a holy symbol. He knows she never explained it. He does not know if she never explained it because there was nothing to explain, or because the explanation was not something she was ready to give, or because she was protecting something, or because Aeralon was not someone she felt she could tell, or because she intended to explain it eventually and ran out of time.

He found her crest carved into the walls of a sealed ruin beneath Grainfall, woven into the royal bloodline of a civilization that predates recorded history. He does not know if she knew this. He does not know if the faith she practiced, the light she served, the choice she made to become a cleric of protection and warmth rather than whatever else the Dawnmere line might have led her toward, was a deliberate rejection of something specific or simply the expression of who she was.

He does not know what killed her. Several physicians and clerics came to her aid. Nothing worked. The illness came and then became final without leaving an explanation behind. He does not know if it was ordinary illness that simply could not be treated, or something else entirely. He does not know what The Architect was or what he did at her bedside or whether his presence and her death the following morning are connected or coincidental. He does not know if his father knew.

He does not know if she knew what was coming.

## What He Feels

He loves her. This is not complicated by the not knowing, or not only complicated. You can love someone you do not have enough of. You can grieve a shape rather than a person and still feel the grief as specific rather than abstract.

He thinks about the note in the journal. He has read it many times since that first morning in the cold house with the sunrise coming through the dormer. She wrote to his father about the ancients and about the next generation and about looking up. She added the postscript in handwriting that was slightly less steady, in what must have been her final days, and told Aeralon to show Zeph the joys the sky brings.

She knew she was dying when she wrote that second part. She used what was left of her steadiness to write instructions for someone else's parenting of her son. She thought, in her final days, about Zeph seeing the sky.

He does not know her. He knows enough of her to know that this was exactly who she was. The person who used her last steady handwriting to make sure her son would be shown something beautiful.

He longs for her the way you long for something you never quite had enough of, which is its own particular kind of ache, different from the grief of losing something you fully possessed. He does not know what it would have been like to grow up with her present. He knows only the shape of her absence and that the shape is large and specific and has never fully been filled by anything else.

He wears her amulet. He put it on the morning he left for Grainfall and has not taken it off since. It is the closest he has to her and he knows it is not very close at all.

## What the Campaign Will Tell Him

He does not know this yet. He is going to find out.

What he knows is that the ruin beneath Grainfall contains her family's crest in a civilization that should not know it exists. That his father spent years excavating the edges of something connected to her bloodline and wrote, in a moment of terrible clarity in the middle of madness, that he had found her. That a man came to her bedside the night before she died and gave a title instead of a name. That the amulet she wore her entire life woke up in the ceremonial room of her ancestors when something spoke to her son in her voice.

She is at the center of this. He does not yet know how or why or what it means that the woman he remembers as warmth and prayer and a name said in a particular way is also apparently woven into something ancient and vast and not entirely safe.

He is going to find out. Whatever it costs him, and he suspects it will cost him something, he is going to find out.

She deserves to be known. Not just longed for. Known.

That is the other reason he is in Grainfall. The quietest one. The one he has not said aloud to anyone.

He wants to know his mother.
