# Session 0

## The Three Questions

---

### 1. Why did you go into the ruin?

Aeralon's journal was dense and meticulous in his earlier years. Precise field notes, careful sketches, the work of a brilliant and methodical mind. But toward the end the pages changed. The handwriting loosened, then fractured, then devolved into scribbles that covered margins and bled across diagrams. References to old gods, to rituals, to a place that matched descriptions Zeph had only ever seen in academic texts. A ruin considered lost. Considered mythological by most.

And then, in the middle of it all, a single sentence written in perfect clarity. Clean and steady, as though the hand that wrote it had briefly returned to itself from somewhere very far away.

> _"I found her, my love. After 20 years. Sylara is here with me."_

That was what sent him through the door. Not scholarship. Not the Dreamrot he was beginning to notice in himself. Not even the duty he felt to finish his father's work. He went in because his father had seen his mother, and Zeph needed to understand what that meant.

---

### 2. What did you see that disturbed you?

The ruin was extraordinary. Untouched, ancient, perfectly preserved in the way that only truly forgotten places are. Artifacts thick with dust. Crypts lining the walls with skeletons still clad in armor from centuries past, jeweled and armed and dressed in designs no living scholar had ever documented firsthand. And on every wall, in every hallway and chamber, murals. Detailed and deliberate, describing events and people and moments from a civilization that history had swallowed whole.

He found the ceremonial room deep inside. A formal space, broad and vaulted, with braziers and altars arranged on a central platform. The walls here told a different story. Not events but lineage. A family tree rendered in careful detail, tracing fathers of kings and mothers of queens, cousins and distant relations sprawling outward across the stone in an unbroken web of bloodline.

And there, woven into that web, was his mother's family crest.

He recognized it immediately. He had seen it his entire life on Sylara's side of the family. It was unmistakable. He stood looking at it for a long moment and then, with shaking hands, he opened the journal.

He found the crest inside. Drawn over and over again, different sizes and orientations, some careful and some frantic. Every single one defaced. Crossed out, etched through, lines torn into the page with what must have been fury or fear or something that had stopped being one and become the other. His father had found this room too. Had stood in this same spot. Had drawn what he saw until he could not stop drawing it and then destroyed every trace of it he could reach.

And surrounding the defaced crests, threaded through the incoherent scribbles, were references to old gods. To rituals. To something ancient connected to this place and this bloodline that Aeralon had clearly understood and just as clearly did not want his son to find. Beside passages written in a language no living scholar had catalogued, in handwriting still steady enough to be deliberate, his father had written a single annotation.

_As far as I can tell this is the language of the brothers. It is called Sae-th. It binds the world._

It was not the crest on the wall that disturbed him. It was the realization settling over him slowly, standing in that room, that his father had not been mad. He had been desperately, urgently trying to leave a message. Every defaced crest, every warning scrawled in the margins, every incoherent page had been a man racing against his own fracturing mind to build a wall between his son and this place.

The message was clear, now that Zeph was standing exactly where his father had not wanted him to go.

_Do not go further._

---

### 3. What did you do that got you arrested?

He did not stop.

His father had written of this room in his earlier years, before the madness. Clean and precise descriptions of a ceremonial space matching this one exactly, cross referenced with a ritual Aeralon had found in texts so obscure they were considered fiction by most scholars. A ritual of the dead. A calling, designed to reach gods long forgotten by the living world.

Aeralon had been infected when he stood here. Aeralon had been losing himself. Zeph was not. He was clear headed, making clean connections, reading the room the way his father had taught him to read every room. He understood what he was looking at. And he understood, with the particular certainty of someone who has spent his life being his father's son, that his father had been too afraid to finish what he started.

He performed the ritual alone in the ceremonial room.

Something spoke back.

A woman's voice. At first he thought it was his mother. The timbre of it, the cadence, something in the shape of the sound reached directly into his earliest memories and pulled. But it was not her. It was something that knew exactly how to sound like her, something that had found the most unguarded place in him and pressed itself against it like a key against a lock.

It asked him to become its servant.

He ran.

No answer, no refusal, simply ran. The room erupted behind him. Skeletons rising from crypts, walls shaking, braziers igniting, urns shattering against the stone floor. The ground heaved beneath him the entire length of the ruin and he did not slow once, did not look back, ran straight through the entrance and into the street outside and grabbed the first person he found and told them that a god had awoken in the ruins beneath Grainfall.

When the authorities went to look, the ruin was exactly as it had always been. Cobwebs intact across every surface. Urns unbroken. Skeletons resting peacefully in their crypts. No sign of disturbance. No sign of anyone having been inside at all.

He was arrested for trespassing in a protected site and, in the eyes of everyone who heard his account, for being dangerously and publicly out of his mind.

He knows what he heard. He knows what it felt like. And he knows that he did not say no.

He simply ran. And whatever is waiting in that bloodline, in that room, in the voice that knew exactly how to sound like Sylara Dawnmere, it knows that too.

---

### What he did not notice until the cell

He did not notice it in the ruins. He was running. The chaos of it, the noise and the heat of the braziers and the sound of stone grinding against stone, left no room for anything small and quiet.

But sometime in the dark of the cell, after the guards had gone and the noise of the street outside had faded, the journal moved.

Not much. A tremor along the cover, like something breathing inside it.

Then a raven pulled itself out of the pages like smoke finding a shape. It landed on his knee, ruffled its feathers once, and looked at him with eyes that carried something he almost recognized. Not his mother. Not the voice from the ritual. Something older and closer and quieter than either.

It did not speak at first. When it did, it spoke in his father's cadences. Fragments. Observations about stonework. Notes about burial rites in the third age. Half finished sentences from field journals Zeph had never been allowed to read.

Aeralon had a familiar his entire career. A companion for every dark corridor and sealed antechamber, every ruin that did not want to be found. Zeph had never seen it. He had never thought to ask where it went when his father started pushing him away.

It had been in the journal the whole time. Waiting in the only place it had left to wait. Not for the ritual in that room, not for the voice or the chaos or the arrest. Waiting for Zeph to be alone and still and quiet enough to notice.

His inheritance was never just the journal. It was what lived inside it.

He does not yet understand what the familiar knows, or how much of his father it carries with it, or whether what woke in that ceremonial room had anything to do with its emergence. Those are questions for later.

For now it is sitting on his knee in a cell in Grainfall, and it is the first living thing that has looked at him since his father died without expecting him to explain himself.

That is enough. For now, that is enough.

## The Core Motivating Question

> _"What would your character do if they discovered something that could change the world — but at a cost to themselves?"_

He would not pursue it and he would not retreat from it.

He has watched what pursuit looks like. His father ran toward the dark until it swallowed him, page by page, year by year, until the man who had closed a journal on every dig to show his son something wonderful became something that pushed his son away and ran toward death in ways that were unlike his character. Aeralon did not choose the cost. He simply kept going until the cost chose him.

He has watched what retreat looks like too. His mother looked at what her bloodline was and what it asked of the people who carried it and chose differently. She built her life in the opposite direction, became a cleric of holy light, wore the mark of her inheritance as a holy symbol and made it mean something else. She paid a different cost. Whether she chose it or whether it was taken from him is a question Zeph does not yet have the answer to. He knows only that a man with a warm smile and circular glasses walked into her room the night before she died, gave his name as The Architect when a curious child asked, and was gone before morning.

Neither of them got to decide on their own terms. That is what he carries. Not their answers. Their cautionary tales.

So his answer is not yes and it is not no. It is not heroic and it is not wise. It is the answer of someone who has inherited two different kinds of loss and is trying to learn from both of them at the same time.

He would stand still. He would not be moved by fear or by hunger. He would look at the thing clearly, the way his mother looked at her own inheritance, without flinching, without reaching. He would wait until he understood what it actually cost and who it would actually cost and whether the world it would change was one worth changing at that price.

And then he would decide. Deliberately. As himself. Not as his father's obsession and not as his mother's sacrifice. As Zephyrix Skydelver, who is twenty three years old and freshly alone and has not yet earned all the wisdom he is reaching for but is reaching for it anyway.

He reacted once in that ceremonial room. He ran from a voice that asked him a question he was not ready to answer. He resents that. His mother would not have run. She would have stood in that room and looked at whatever was speaking and made her choice with both eyes open.

He is going back. And this time he is going to answer.

Whatever it costs him, he will have chosen it. That is the only thing he knows for certain. The cost is coming regardless. The only question is whether he meets it on his own terms or lets it collect without his consent the way it collected from everyone else who carried this name.

He will stand still. He will decide. He will not react.

That is all he has. For now it is enough.

## The Shared Rumor Exercise

_Three things people say about the ruins. What Zeph thinks when he hears them._

---

### "The ruins were built to worship something beneath the world."

He has stood in that ceremonial room. He has seen the altars and the braziers and the family tree carved into the walls with his mother's crest woven into the bloodline of something ancient enough to predate recorded history. He has read his father's annotation about Sae-th, the language of the brothers, the thing that binds the world.

Worship is the wrong word. He does not say this out loud, not yet, not to people who have not been inside. But he thinks it every time he hears this rumor.

The room was not built for devotion. It was built for something more specific than that. The braziers and altars and the arrangement of the space, he knows ceremonial architecture, his father taught him to read a room the way other fathers teach children to read words, and what that room described was not prayer. It was contract. It was a space designed for an agreement between parties, for the formalization of something that had already been decided somewhere else.

You do not worship what you have a contract with. You serve it. Or you break from it. Or it collects.

He keeps this thought close. It is not ready to be said aloud yet. But it is the most important thing he knows about the ruins and he knows it because his mother's crest was on that wall and she spent her life becoming the opposite of what it represented.

She did not worship something beneath the world. She refused it. And something came to her bedside the night before she died with a warm smile and circular glasses.

The ruins were not built to worship. They were built to remember an agreement. And someone is still honoring it.

---

### "The ancient civilization was destroyed by divine punishment."

His first instinct is the scholar's instinct. Civilizations are rarely destroyed by anything divine. They are destroyed by resource collapse, by disease, by internal fracture, by being on the wrong side of a migration pattern. The gods get credit for a lot of things that were already happening.

His second instinct, the one that comes after standing in that ceremonial room with his father's journal open to a page he was never meant to read, is more complicated.

Divine punishment implies something was done wrong. It implies a standard that was violated, a line that was crossed, a power that decided enough was enough. That framing assumes the destroying force was righteous and the destroyed civilization was not.

But his mother's crest was on that wall. And his mother was the most righteous person he ever knew. She was light and warmth and faith and choice and she wore her bloodline's mark as a holy symbol and made it mean something better than what it was given to her to mean.

If her ancestors were destroyed by something calling itself divine, he wants to know who decided that. He wants to know what they did that warranted it. He wants to know if the destroying force and the thing that spoke to him in the ceremonial room are the same entity or different ones.

He does not say any of this. He simply nods when he hears the rumor and files it under things that need more information before he has an opinion worth sharing.

What he knows is that divine punishment is a story told by whoever survived. And survivors have reasons for the stories they tell.

---

### "People who enter the ruins don't come back... or come back wrong."

He came back. He is sitting in a jail cell in Grainfall with a raven on his knee and his mother's pendant glowing softly against his chest and he came back. That is something.

Whether he came back right is a question he is less certain about. He noticed the changes before he went into the ruin. Small things, subtle enough to dismiss, familiar enough to recognize because he had watched them happen to someone else for years. He knows this pattern. He watched it hollow out the most important person in his life over the course of a decade and he does not know yet whether what he is noticing in himself is the same thing or something adjacent to it or something else entirely.

His father went into those ruins and came back wrong over years. Slowly, incrementally, in ways that were easy to explain away until they were not. The reclusiveness, the bitterness, the anger that had no target, the way he pushed everyone away, the way he ran toward death in ways that were unlike his character.

Zeph went into the ruins once and ran out again in under an hour. He does not know what that means for the trajectory. He does not know if the brevity of the exposure matters or if the ritual changed the calculation entirely. He does not know if the raven and the amulet activating in the same night is connected or coincidental.

What he knows is that he hears this rumor and he does not dismiss it the way the people saying it expect him to. He hears it and he thinks about his father. He thinks about the journal pages that grew more erratic as they approached the present. He thinks about a single sentence written in perfect clarity in the middle of madness.

And then he thinks about himself. About what he is noticing. About the pattern he recognizes.

He came back. He is reasonably certain of that.

He came back right is the question he is going to spend this campaign answering.
